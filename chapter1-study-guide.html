Chapter 1: Introduction to Multinational Financial Management

Comprehensive study notes with key concepts, definitions, and takeaways

1 Financial Management: Background
▾
Definition: Financial management is the management of the cost of borrowing and the returns on investment. A good finance manager strives to borrow at a lower cost and invest in higher-return investments.

Scope of Financial Management

  • Personal finance management
  • Business finance management (from small businesses to global corporations)

Forms of Business Organization

  • Sole Proprietorship - owned by one individual
  • Partnership - owned by two or more individuals
  • Corporations - (a) domestic, (b) international
Key Point: Financial management applies at every scale - from managing your personal budget to managing global MNCs. The core principle remains the same: minimize borrowing costs, maximize investment returns.
2 The Multinational Corporation (MNC)
▾
Definition: An MNC is a company with production and distribution facilities in more than one country.

Key Characteristics of MNCs

  • MNCs are in a better position to exploit different costs and skills between nations
  • They transmit international competition across borders
  • They bring production efficiencies to the markets they operate in

Reasons for Going International

  • To seek raw materials - access to natural resources not available domestically
  • To seek more markets - expand customer base beyond domestic borders
  • To minimize costs of production - leverage lower labor, land, or operational costs abroad
Key Point: MNCs create value by arbitraging differences in costs, skills, and resources across countries while increasing competition and efficiency.
3 Goals of the MNC
▾

Theoretical Goals (Multiple possible goals)

  • Social welfare
  • Generating jobs
  • Welfare of workers and their families
  • Protecting the environment
  • Minimisation of costs
  • Maximisation of sales
  • Minimisation of risk
  • Maximisation of shareholders' wealth
  • Maximisation of accounting profits
Critical Question: Can an MNC work to achieve ALL of these goals simultaneously? The answer is no - these goals often conflict with each other.

The Primary Goal

Primary Goal: The commonly accepted goal of MNCs is to maximize shareholder wealth. No matter what other goals exist, they cannot be achieved long-term if shareholders (owners) are not looked after.
  • Focus on MNCs that wholly own their foreign subsidiaries
  • Financial managers throughout the MNC have a single goal: maximizing the value of the entire MNC
4 Constraints Interfering with the MNC Goal
▾

While the primary goal is shareholder wealth maximization, MNCs face constraints that can interfere with this objective, commonly framed as ESG factors:

Environmental Constraints (E)

  • Environmental regulations and sustainability requirements
  • Carbon emissions and pollution controls
  • Resource conservation mandates

Social Constraints (S)

  • Labor laws and worker welfare
  • Community impact and social responsibility
  • Human rights compliance

Governance Constraints (G)

  • Corporate governance standards
  • Transparency and reporting requirements
  • Board structure and accountability
Key Point: ESG constraints represent a balancing act - MNCs must pursue shareholder wealth while satisfying environmental, social, and governance obligations in each country they operate.
5 Theories of International Business
▾

Three key theories justify why firms engage in international business:

1. Theory of Comparative Advantage

  • Countries should specialize in producing goods where they have a comparative advantage
  • Trade benefits all parties when each country focuses on what it produces most efficiently
  • Explains why international trade is mutually beneficial

2. Theory of Imperfect Markets Theory

  • Markets for factors of production (labor, capital, technology) are imperfect
  • Imperfections create opportunities for firms to capitalize on cost and skill differences
  • Explains why MNCs exist - they exploit market imperfections across borders

3. Product Life Cycle Theory

  • Products go through stages: introduction, growth, maturity, and decline
  • Firms initially produce domestically, then expand internationally as the product matures
  • Explains the sequential internationalization of firms
  • As products mature, production moves to lower-cost countries
Key Point: These three theories complement each other - comparative advantage explains trade patterns, imperfect markets explain MNC existence, and the product cycle explains the timing of international expansion.
6 Methods of International Business (Part 1)
▾

Firms engage in international business through six main methods. Here are the first three:

1. International Trade (Export/Import)

  • Relatively conservative approach to penetrate new and large markets (exporting)
  • Obtain supplies at a low cost (importing)
  • Minimal risk - no capital at risk
  • The internet facilitates international trade by allowing firms to advertise and accept orders online

2. Licensing

Definition: Licensing obligates a firm to provide its technology (copyrights, patents, trademarks, or trade names) in exchange for fees or other specified benefits.
  • Allows firms to use their technology in foreign markets without major investment
  • No transportation costs that result from exporting
  • Major disadvantage: Difficult to ensure quality control in the foreign production process

3. Franchising

Definition: Franchising obligates a firm to provide a specialized sales or service strategy, support assistance, and possibly an initial investment in exchange for periodic fees.
  • Allows penetration into foreign markets without a major investment
  • Example: McDonald's (McD) franchise model
7 Methods of International Business (Part 2)
▾

The remaining three methods of engaging in international business:

4. Joint Ventures

Definition: A venture jointly owned and operated by two or more firms. A firm may enter a foreign market by engaging in a joint venture with firms that reside in those markets.
  • Allows two firms to apply their respective cooperative advantages in a given project
  • Example: Myvi (Malaysian car joint venture)
  • Combines local market knowledge with foreign expertise

5. Acquisitions of Existing Operations

  • Acquiring firms in foreign countries allows full control over foreign businesses
  • Quickly obtains a large portion of foreign market share
  • Risk: Subject to large losses because of larger investment required
  • Risk: Liquidation may be difficult if the foreign subsidiary performs poorly

6. Establishing New Foreign Subsidiaries

  • Firms penetrate markets by establishing new operations in foreign countries
  • Requires a large investment to start from ground zero
  • Operations can be tailored exactly to the firm's needs
  • May require smaller investment than buying an existing firm
  • Requires more country risk investigations
Summary of Methods: The six methods range from low risk/low control (trade) to high risk/high control (new subsidiaries). Firms choose based on their risk appetite, investment capacity, and strategic goals.
8 Uncertainties & Chapter Summary
▾

Uncertainties Surrounding MNC Cash Flow

MNCs face three major types of uncertainty that affect their cash flows:

  • Exposure to international economic conditions: If economic conditions in a foreign country weaken, purchases of products decline and MNC sales in that country may be lower than expected.
  • Exposure to international political risk: A foreign government may increase taxes or impose barriers on the MNC's subsidiary.
  • Exposure to exchange rate risk: If foreign currencies related to the MNC subsidiary weaken against the U.S. dollar, the MNC will receive a lower amount of dollar cash flows than expected.
Key Point: These three risks - economic, political, and exchange rate - are interconnected and can compound each other, making risk management a critical function for MNCs.
